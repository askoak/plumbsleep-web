Sleep, aligned.

A modular sleep-alignment system for adult side sleepers. Pre-prototype. Architecture ratified. Stage 1 wear test materials ready to execute. Capital required to launch: ~$21,000. Bottom-up visible-market floor: ~$58M/yr (knee/leg-pillow category, sample of 29 ASINs; true category materially larger). Side-venture profit profile: $42k–$168k annual contribution at 1,500–6,000 units/yr at premium-functional price tier.

This is a confidential investor brief for Plumb Sleep and parties Plumb has explicitly approved. Not for redistribution.

1. The opportunity — one paragraph

The US knee/leg-pillow category is large, fragmented, and commodity-dominated. The visible 29-ASIN slice we tracked drives roughly $58 million in annual revenue (knee/leg pillows alone, excluding body-pillow substitutes that capture another ~$27M from this set); the full US category, with hundreds of SKUs, is in the low-to-mid hundreds of millions per published sleep-category estimates. Every leading product fails at specific, repeated, fixable things: sizing (the #1 complaint in every tier we coded, 18–25%), retention (a bimodal failure on strap-based products), comfort and cooling (the reason buyers defect to body pillows), and foam durability. Plumb is a three-component modular wearable — a viscose-made-from-bamboo knit thigh sleeve, a removable washable cover, and a contoured hourglass foam pillow — designed against those four named failures with body-sized fit, anatomy-anchored retention (no strap), CertiPUR-US foam, and a 1-year customer guarantee (full refund, free shipping both ways). Premium-functional, not luxury. Capturing 0.5–1% of just the visible knee/leg slice is $290,000–$580,000 in annual revenue — a credible lucrative side venture at low overhead.

2. What we know with evidence (and what we don't)

This brief is built on a saturated buyer-voice corpus: 4,962 unique Amazon reviews across five product tiers (premium knee, value knee, bolster/half-moon, wearable/strap, and body/pregnancy-pillow substitutes — the products buyers actually defect to). Reviews were balanced between positive and critical (2,604 / 2,358), LLM-coded against a unified taxonomy with a saturation and coverage validator, and stress-tested with retrieval-augmented querying. Working notes and reproducible scripts are in research/18 through research/24 in the private project repository.

Plumb's founder is a mathematical statistician who has built and deployed proprietary AI retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems for market research, evidence synthesis, and decision-support analytics across unrelated domains. He is also a long-time side sleeper who has used a knee pillow for years and has lived with the category's complaint set firsthand — sizing failure, retention failure, comfort and cooling shortfalls — alongside chronic back-pain context that initially drove the search for a better solution. The corpus analysis confirms what personal experience suggested: no incumbent solves both halves of the central design tension.

What the corpus tells us with confidence:

  • The category's failure pattern at scale (sizing #1, retention #2 on wearables, comfort/cooling #3, foam durability #4, washability modest #5)
  • The competitive thesis (no incumbent solves the medical-aesthetic-vs-staying-in-place tension structurally)
  • The defection vector (buyers leave knee pillows for body pillows on comfort and cooling, accepting worse positioning)
  • Bottom-up market floor (29-ASIN revealed-preference revenue estimate)

What the corpus cannot tell us — and the next-dollar tests do:

  • Whether anatomy-anchored retention actually works in a real sleeper through a 30-night cycle (Gate 1 wear test answers this for ~$1,500)
  • Whether the modular design clears Pillow Solve US 10,143,311 B2 + Contour USD846312 + five additional patent and trademark threats counsel needs to vet (Gate 2 FTO clearance answers this for ~$1,500–4,000)
  • Exact willingness-to-pay at the chosen price point (requires a live-waitlist concept/price test; this is a launch action and remains Mike-gated for a separate decision)

3.5 Mechanical rationale

The product description above states what Plumb is. This section states why each engineering decision is what it is. The intended reader is someone who will ask how the forces work, where the sizing numbers come from, and why the foam spec is what it is. The answers are below, with sources.

3.5.1 Retention mechanism — decoupling friction from cushion

Every wearable knee-pillow product in the corpus uses one of two retention strategies: a strap that wraps the cushion to the leg (Helix, Contour, the strap-style wearable tier), or no retention at all (the loose-pillow majority). Both strategies couple retention force to cushion firmness. A strap tight enough to keep the cushion in place compresses the cushion against the inner thigh; a soft cushion that is comfortable against the thigh is not held tightly enough by the strap to survive a side-to-prone-to-side rotation.

Plumb decouples retention from the cushion by anchoring retention to the thigh circumference rather than the pillow geometry. The thigh sleeve is a load-bearing structural element — it produces a normal force distributed around the upper-thigh circumference via fabric tension plus discrete silicone-dot grippers at the interior top rim. The covered pillow is then attached to the sleeve at the inner-thigh contact zone via the Stage-1-arm-selected mechanism (magnetic, snap, or pouch — package/40 v6 Track 6 selects). The pillow's job is comfort; the sleeve's job is staying. The mechanical separation is the design.

This matters because the dominant failure mode in the wearable tier of the buyer corpus is not "the cushion is uncomfortable" — it is "the strap holds when I'm still and fails when I move." (Corpus: research/01 §"Doesn't Work for Position-Shifters," research/07 §Helix, §Contour.) A retention surface distributed over the full upper-thigh circumference of a knit sleeve produces ~3–4× the skin-contact area of a strap of equivalent compression force, reducing the peak contact pressure proportionately and allowing significantly lower compression to achieve the same retention reliability. The skin-pressure budget is the limiting variable in any wearable that contacts a sleep surface for 6–9 hours; spreading the load is the design lever.

3.5.2 Sizing rationale — two SKUs from the thigh-circumference distribution

The Regular (≤28 in. / ≤71 cm upper-thigh) and XL (28–32 in. / 71–81 cm) sleeve sizing is bottom-up from US adult anthropometric data, not vendor convention.

Source: National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1988–1994 thigh-circumference reference tables, US adults age 20+, all race/ethnicity groups. The published 5th-to-95th-percentile range for adult US males spans ~17.3 in. (44.0 cm) to ~24.1 in. (61.2 cm); for adult US females, the comparable range is ~17.4 in. (44.2 cm) to ~25.6 in. (65.0 cm). NHANES 2010-2012 shifts both distributions upward (per the IISE anthropometric-change webinar, 2016). The upper 95th-percentile target in present-day adult civilian populations runs to ~26–28 in. The Regular SKU (≤28 in.) therefore captures the full NHANES-1988 distribution and the great majority of the present-day distribution; the XL SKU (28–32 in.) extends to roughly the 99th percentile of US adult female and ~97th percentile of US adult male thigh circumference.

Two sleeve sizes were chosen instead of one (the category default) or five (the apparel default). One sleeve at a single circumference would force grip compromise at either tail — too loose for the smaller end (no retention) or too tight for the larger end (skin-pressure exceedance). Five sleeves would fragment inventory, inflate MOQ across SKUs, and add fit-decision friction at purchase. Two SKUs at the midpoint split give >95% fit coverage on NHANES-1988 data with two part numbers — a deliberately lean SKU lineup chosen for production economics, not aesthetic minimalism.

3.5.3 Foam spec — ILD 20–28 for the side-sleeper inter-thigh load

The pillow foam is open-cell polyurethane, CertiPUR-US certified, with Indentation Force Deflection (IFD, the standardized industry name for ILD) in the 20–28 lb-force range at 25% compression on the 50-square-inch standard indenter.

The 20–28 IFD band sits at the medium-soft-to-medium boundary of the foam firmness scale. The selection rationale: in lateral-decubitus (side-lying) posture, the inter-thigh foam pillow is loaded across a contact zone of roughly 35–50 square inches and supports a fraction of the upper leg's weight modulated by hip flexion angle — a fraction of approximately 8–12 lb of distributed load at typical adult body weight, not the 80–120 lb load a mattress comfort layer sees from full torso weight. The pressure-relief mattress literature for side sleepers indicates lower-ILD comfort layers (12–18 IFD) for full-body-weight pressure points; the Plumb pillow sits at a different load regime (lower magnitude, smaller contact area) and is spec'd accordingly. Reference: Effects of Mattress Material on Body Pressure Profiles, Low et al., Journal of Chiropractic Medicine, 2016 (side-sleeper torso and buttock pressure-distribution analysis).

The optional 6-inch thickness SKU (vs. the 5-inch baseline) is offered for larger frames and firmer mattresses, where compressed inter-thigh distance is greater. Foam thickness was not raised universally to 6 inches because the incremental height shifts hip alignment past neutral for shorter-femur sleepers; the two-thickness SKU lineup is the same anthropometric-coverage logic as the two-circumference sleeve lineup.

See Figure 1 — Inter-thigh loading geometry, side-lying posture.

3.5.4 Attachment mechanism — the variable Stage 1 resolves

The sleeve geometry, pillow shape, and material specs above are held constant through Stage 1. The single variable evaluated is the attachment mechanism between the covered pillow and the sleeve. Three concepts advance to the wear-test arm-selector per package/40 v6:

  • Arm A — magnetic snap interface. Embedded magnets in the cover-back panel and the sleeve exterior at the inner-thigh contact zone. Failure modes evaluated: hold-through-the-night, one-handed release, magnet edge sharpness on skin, wash survival.
  • Arm B — kam-snap tab. Fabric tabs on the cover engaging kam-snaps on the sleeve exterior. Failure modes: engagement-force-vs-release-force balance, hair-pull risk, snap retention through wash.
  • Arm C — sleeve-mounted exterior pouch. A structured open-top pouch on the sleeve exterior; the covered pillow slides in from the top. Failure modes: pouch-mouth elastic recovery after wash, retention force at the open top edge, awkwardness of one-handed insertion.

Velcro is excluded by design inference from the buyer-corpus bathroom-friction evidence (Contour Legacy non-removable strap "cited as cumbersome for bathroom trips during the night" — research/07:139).

See Figure 2 — Attachment-mechanism family tree.

The full kill-criteria per arm (cycles, seconds, ratings, hair-pull/skin-pinch gates, the "2 a.m. inversion" disruption rating) are summarized in §7 below under Gate 1.

7.1 Gate 1 protocol detail

The brief above states what Gate 1 costs and what it answers. Engineering readers will reasonably want to see the protocol structure before forming an opinion. The full protocol is package/40 v6; the summary below is the investor-readable extract.

Stage 1 wear test — design summary
Cohort 4 testers, recruited from Mike's personal network or substantive feedback-form responses (not anonymous-paid; tester quality is the lever, not tester count).
Duration 30 consecutive nights per tester. No skip days except for illness or travel.
Design Within-subject. Each tester rotates through all three attachment arms (magnetic / snap / pouch) in randomized 10-night blocks. Sleeve and pillow are identical across all three arms — only the attachment mechanism differs.
Six measurement tracks
  1. Sleeve fit & rotation — time-lapse on ≥5 nights; kill on >15° rotation or >2 in. vertical migration in 2+ testers on 2+ nights.
  2. 30-night tolerance — daily wear yes/no; kill on >3 abandoned nights per tester for 2+ testers.
  3. Bathroom-break ergonomics — per-event log of one-handed detach/reattach time and "frictionless" rating; kill an arm at median detach >5s, median reattach >10s, or any tester needing two hands more than 1-in-5 events.
  4. Wash durability — sleeve, cover, and arm-specific hardware rated through 30 wash cycles (10 in situ + 20 bench); kill on any rating dropping to 1–2 in first 15 cycles.
  5. Pillow shape retention — photo-and-rating at nights 1/7/14/21/30; pass ≥3.5 cohort-mean shape rating and no worsening comfort trend.
  6. Attachment reliability (arm-selector) — hold (≤1 unintended detachment per 10 nights), release (≥4 of 5 eyes-closed one-handed trials succeed, median ≤5s), cycle durability (30 bench cycles, no rating drop below 3), safety (zero hair-pull / skin-pinch events), and the "2 a.m. inversion" experiential test — of any detachment events, ≤80% may register as waking, median disruption ≤2 (1–5 scale), and zero may register as a fall/hazard.
Decision rule An arm must pass tracks 3, 4-hardware, and 6 to remain a candidate. Among surviving arms, the winner is the highest composite of friction + wash-survival + hold/release/durability/safety, with hold and safety weighted as binary gates (an arm that ever pinches skin or pulls hair cannot win regardless of composite score).
Pass standard At least one arm clears all six tracks across all four testers. Zero arms passing = Gate 1 fails, the design is held until re-engineered.
Source protocol package/40 v6

This is not a vibe-check or a one-tester pilot. The protocol structure is a small within-subject trial with parallel bench durability, kill criteria that are quantitative and pre-registered, and an explicit failure-mode ("2 a.m. inversion") that the quantitative measures cannot by themselves capture. The same design principles a Phase-1 medical-device feasibility study would use, sized appropriately for a comfort product.

8. Risks (honest)

A fiduciary-readable risk list. Mitigations named where they exist.

  • Retention is unproven. This is the make-or-break risk. Gate 1 is the binary test. Mitigation: spend $1,500 to know.
  • FTO under modular is tighter than under integrated. The modular pivot reads structurally closer to Pillow Solve's "case, cover, or strap" attachment claim than an integrated design would. Counsel determination required. Mitigation: Gate 2 attorney review before tooling.
  • Channel discipline. Paid-social-first loses money at this price point and scale. Plumb's plan must remain organic/Amazon-led; deviation kills the unit economics. Mitigation: documented channel sequence in package/90; gate transitions explicit.
  • Manufacturing complexity. Three components plus attachment is harder than a single foam pillow. First-article QC matters; supplier selection matters. Mitigation: package/87 BOM is explicit on supplier criteria; Stage 2+ adds molded-hourglass-contour capability screen and two-IFD-tier evaluation.
  • Tariff exposure. Section 301 tariffs on China-origin knit fabric could add ~$1.50/unit at 25% rate, compressing margin by ~3.3 percentage points at $69 retail. Mitigation: package/87 §7a requires HTS classification analysis and Vietnam/Indonesia alternative-origin cost comparison before Stage 2 manufacturing commitment.
  • No luxury premium available. The corpus evidence is unambiguous: aesthetic-led luxury demand does not exist in this category. Plumb must earn its $69 premium through materials, quality, and function — not through brand prestige. A Saatva / Brooklinen / luxury-bedding play is not the playbook.
  • Founder time. The founder maintains a separate primary professional practice. Plumb is a side venture and must remain so. Sales-effort time (clinical-channel introductions) competes with the day job; capital downside is bounded but attention downside is real.
  • Buyer trust as a category problem. "Not as described" runs 10–23% across tiers (worst on bolster + substitute tiers). An honest 1-year customer guarantee with free returns both ways is a real differentiator; broken or hedged execution of it is a brand-killer.

9. What this investment is not

  • Not a luxury brand build. Plumb is premium-functional. The corpus does not support a luxury positioning; pricing at $100+ is unevidenced demand.
  • Not a venture-scale company. Side-venture profit profile, not a Casper-scale unicorn play. Capital is bounded; capital intensity stays low.
  • Not a medical device. No medical claims, no antimicrobial claims, no patent-pending claims. FTC general-comfort framing only. Net-impression review standard applies to every customer-facing artifact (package/86 §Net-impression review standard).
  • Not a discretionary impulse purchase. This is a sleep-positioning aid bought by people who have an actual side-sleep alignment problem and have tried two or three solutions that didn't work.
